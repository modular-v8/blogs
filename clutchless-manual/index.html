Clutchless Manuals Halfway to an Automatic

01

What is a clutchless-manual transmission?

It is still very much a manual transmission, with a regular clutch. The term "clutchless" simply means that the driver doesn't operate the clutch, because there is no clutch pedal. It is not an automatic gearbox. It just aims to relieve the left leg of the person driving a manual car.

Only the clutch is operated automatically. The regular H-pattern gear lever stays, and it still moves the selector forks mechanically inside the gearbox, so choosing the gear remains entirely the driver's job. That is the line between a clutchless manual and an AMTs: an AMT automates the clutch and the gear selection, so its lever is a request, not a command. Here, the lever is still a lever.

Currently, this is used by Hyundai and Kia, who offer it as an option alongside the conventional manual, under the name iMT, which stands for Intelligent Manual Transmission. Hyundai has it on the Venue and i20, Kia on the Sonet, Seltos and Carens Clavis, mostly in India. On the Sonet's turbo-petrol, in fact, you can only pick between the iMT and a DCT. There is no conventional manual at all.

If you haven't heard of such transmissions, it's not your fault. But, it is by no means a novel idea. The South Koreans have revived a concept that has been invented, and reinvented, many times before. Over roughly seven decades, well over a dozen manufacturers have tried to bridge the gap between the "cumbersome" manual and the full-blown automatic, with something like twenty different systems between them. But, none of them ever managed to catch the fancy of the majority of drivers and ended up being forgotten.

Let's unturn a few stones then.

02

How does it work today, and how did it work back then?

Today's electronics are good enough to make some of the best automatic transmissions we have ever known. The actuations, gearshifts are fast, smooth and reliable. Clutchless manuals borrow a very small slice of that technology. Sensors work out what the driver is up to, and a computer commands an electro-hydraulic actuator to disengage and re-engage the clutch at the right moment, every time. In the iMT, a sensor on the gear lever tells the control unit that a shift is coming, the control unit builds hydraulic pressure, and that pressure travels down a tube to a slave cylinder wrapped around the clutch shaft. The gearbox behind it is a stock six-speed manual.

But, as said before, this idea has been around for a long time, and so have the two problems it has to solve: how to sense the intent to change gear, and how to actually move the clutch. The answers to both have changed a lot over the decades.

The first systems appeared in the 1940s and 50s, long before anyone could put a computer in a car. Check out the timeline below to see how, when and who did what.

Sensing the driver's intent

It is not that difficult to operate a clutch with a computer. What's more difficult is letting the car know when to operate it. How can the driver's intent to change gear be detected reliably, every time?

Obviously, part of this detection has always been built into the gear lever itself. Mercedes' Hydrak in 1957 used a simple microswitch at the base of the shift lever, and Porsche and Volkswagen a decade later moved the switch into the gear knob. Modern iMT still uses a so called "intention sensor" on the lever, and Ford has even patented a pressure sensor in the knob that lets you modulate the clutch by squeezing it (quite strange, no wonder there are no real-world applications on this patent yet).

But as you might imagine, a system relying only on the lever would be quite dumb. What if the driver shifts down into a gear that would overrev the engine and damage it? That is why the smarter systems couple the lever with additional inputs from sensors all over the vehicle. Saab's Sensonic in the 1990s also read accelerator pressure, and flooring the throttle locked out gear selection altogether. Around the same time, Valeo's electronic clutch on the Ferrari Mondial t cross-checked the lever against engine speed, throttle position, and the speeds of the gearbox input and output shafts. It could even refuse a shift that would harm the engine or gearbox.

Operating the clutch

Before electronics were capable of what they are today, manufacturers used vacuum to generate the force needed to move the clutch. This was quite common. By pulling air from the intake manifold, partial vacuum can be generated in a chamber with diaphragm. By manipulating this, older cars used the force generated by vacuum to operate their pop-up headlights and many other things.

The very early Saxomat, sold by Fichtel & Sachs to a whole host of carmakers from the mid-1950s, combined a centrifugal clutch for pulling away (it only engages above a certain engine speed, and below that it stays disengaged, so the car can sit at idle without stalling) with a vacuum servo for the gear changes. Citroën, on their DS, used its high-pressure hydraulics for the job, thanks to the tremendous force those can deliver. Some even tried playing with electromagnetism, as Renault's Ferlec on the Dauphine and the Subaru 360 did, and AMC ended up using engine oil pressure to control the clutch. By 1960, nearly every way of automatically operating a clutch had already been tried. But, what was missing was anything smart enough to decide how fast or slow to close it.

Some of these early cars also cheated a little. Porsche's Sportomatic and Volkswagen's Automatic Stick Shift removed the clutch pedal but bolted a torque converter in front of the clutch, so the car couldn't stall when stopping. The converter did the hard part (moving off from rest), and the switch in the knob only took care of the clutch during shifts.

In recent times, though, electro-hydraulic actuation has stood the test of time. An electric motor drives a piston, which sends pressure down a closed hydraulic line to the clutch, and sensors measure how far the clutch has travelled. That is how Saab did it in the 90s (an electric motor and worm gear turning the clutch master cylinder), and it is how the iMT still does it today.

03

Why it never took off?

In theory, clutchless manuals offer the good of many worlds. Firstly, they are based on a completely regular manual transmission, with minimal modifications. They aim to remove probably the most cumbersome part of driving a manual, whilst keeping the "feel" of shifting the same. They are only marginally more expensive than a manual, and nowhere near as expensive as an automatic.

Over the past decades, though, each iteration has met its end for its own reasons, and they sound remarkably alike. Mercedes dropped the Hydrak around 1961, after only a few years, because it depended on a combination of vacuum and electrical components that proved failure-prone, and owners were thus unhappy. In the 70s, Porsche's Sportomatic was also phased out after a modest run. It simply never took off with buyers, its performance was slightly behind the ordinary manual, and many cars had their transmissions swapped for the five-speed manual later on.

By the late 90s, Saab and Renault also found themselves shelving their respective clutchless manuals. The Saab Sensonic's public image took a beating from Top Gear, where James May tried to parallel-park one and ended up hitting a parked car. The reason: there is a small delay between releasing the brake and the clutch biting, and that is exactly the kind of slow manoeuvre a clutch pedal exists to modulate. Everything from the sensors on the lever to the electric motor, the hydraulic actuator and the microprocessor had to work perfectly, and a fault anywhere in the chain could leave the car immobile. Repairs were costly, and many owners went back to a normal manual. Renault's Twingo Easy had a similar story, being slow between gears and prone to breakdowns thanks to a flimsy plastic part, before it was replaced by a conventional automatic in 1997. The pattern repeats all the way to Mercedes' A-Class in the 2000s where the actuator was the weak link and a replacement costed more than the car is worth.

Today, Hyundai and Kia still offer their iMTs and, honestly, it does not seem like they are suffering the same fate as the earlier ones. Reviewers find it easy to live with. You cannot stall it and it crawls in traffic without touching the accelerator, just like a manual car. It doesn't have the lazy shifts of an AMT, because they are still in the driver's hands. Moreover, it costs just a negligible amount over the manual (the Venue iMT was only about 20,000 INR more than the equivalent manual when it launched). It isn't flawless, of course. An owner reported three roadside breakdowns in a month on a three-year-old Venue, all traced to parts in the very chain described above (the actuator, the clutch bearing and the clutch tube). That is one owner and not a failure rate, but it shows the weak link has not gone away entirely.

There is one more reason this time is different, and it isn't comfort. Modern iMT is designed to work with a 48V mild-hybrid system. A manual gearbox can't coast with the engine off, because the driver's foot is the only thing that can open the clutch. With the clutch now automated, the engine can switch off early while you coast, which apparently helps to cut CO2 by up to 3% in real-world driving. In other words, earlier attempts removed the pedal for the driver's comfort. This time there's an additional benefit in terms of emissions, at least on paper.

04

Final Verdict

It was interesting to see a concept being introduced and axed multiple times in the past, and now witnessing its revival. Although manual purists still might hate a clutchless manual transmission, you cannot deny that it is not half bad. The fact that it has been selling quietly for the past six years is the proof. But I completely imagine why driving one would feel weird.

In my opinion, it's not the technology, it's the way we are accustomed to our cars that makes trying out and adapting to a new concept tricky and difficult. And it is exactly the reason why depite their strengths, we tend to ignore such innovations.
