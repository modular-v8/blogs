Porsche 917/30 Why were the turbos so far away?

There are a lot of instances where technology used in motorsport has helped to improve road cars, the ones you and I can go out and buy.

The presence of turbochargers in almost all vehicles today is no exception. But before turbocharged engines achieved near perfection, they also had to start somewhere. A few weeks ago, while at the Porsche Museum in Stuttgart, I got to see one of the seeds of it.

But before turbocharged engines achieved near perfection, they also had to start somewhere. A few weeks ago, while at the Porsche Museum in Stuttgart, I got to see one of the seeds of it.

While perusing the amazing history of Porsche, I came across this: the Porsche 917/30. It was a dedicated race car developed for Can-Am (Canada & North America) racing in the early 1970s. The thing that immediately caught my eye, among other things, was the placement of the two turbochargers relative to the massive flat-12-cylinder engine. The first thought that came to my mind was, "You are losing so much boost pressure and intake air pressure by spacing the engine and turbos so far apart!"

I thought surely this setup was not efficient, because, as previously shown in one of my other blogs, turbochargers act like air compressors, and the farther away from the compressor the air travels, the more distance it has to cover and the more energy it subsequently loses while doing so.

So, I decided to dig deeper into this topic.

01

Why turbochargers in the first place?

In the early 1970s, turbochargers were not unheard of in motorsport. America as well as Europe had some experience with them. But not Porsche. So, they took it upon themselves to delve into this concept for their race cars.

The reason? They wanted to focus on showcasing their technology in the American market. Porsche had already been participating in one of the biggest racing championships in North America, the Can-Am series. But now they decided to go all in.

They realized they had no capable engine to compete with their competitors at the time, like McLaren, Lola, Ford and others. So, they came up with the idea of "turbocharging" their existing 4.5-litre engine previously used in the predecessor race car.

But there was a problem. No one at Porsche Germany had any experience in how to develop a turbocharged engine, let alone make it race-worthy.

So, Porsche experimented first with the 917/10 Racecar, before eventually evolving it into the 917/30 version.

02

Why place the turbos so far?

Today, most cars have their turbochargers placed right beside or "within" the engine. But Porsche at that time decided to place them on the opposite end of the car.

There are a few justifiable reasons for it: firstly, they had to lengthen the overall vehicle to improve the stability of the car at high speeds. This, coupled with the fact that the space around the engine was already quite crowded, meant that placing the turbos far away made sense.

With a longer car, it was also important to look after the weight distribution so that there were no negative effects on the handling of the vehicle. That is why the entire turbocharger assembly was pushed to the very back of the vehicle, about a metre away from the engine.

One more advantage might have been avoiding heat-soaking the air-cooled 12 cylinders (Yes - Porsche used air-cooled engines even for their race cars!). With the turbos feeding on very high-temperature exhaust gases, it was sensible to keep them in another territory altogether.

03

The question I have

The thing I was most surprised by was how they managed the efficiency of the turbos if the exhaust gases and pressurized air had to travel a mile between the engine and turbochargers. Yes, Porsche did use wastegates (valves that allow exhaust to escape directly into the atmosphere when turbos are not needed) and a bypass valve (to avoid too much pressurization of intake air), both of which are ubiquitious components of a turbocharged engine today.

I am no expert, but I still believe that in travelling such a long distance, the exhaust gases would lose their energy and thus spool the turbos less. On top of that, the intake air, which is pressurized by the turbos, would lose much of its prescious boost before it reaches the engine, thus robbing potential power gains.

But maybe this is what they learned during their first development of a turbocharged engine. And it paid off because these learnings carried over to the first production Porsche 911 Turbo unveiled in 1974. And today the capability of the "Turbo" badge on the back of a Porsche is well known.
